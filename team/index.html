What we do

Your architect joins one squad for the whole program. Same standups, same deadline.

  1. Turn the business ask into requirements.We run the workshops with your business owners and write requirements your product owner can put straight into the backlog.
  2. Write the design down.Every decision gets written up as an ADR, with the options we turned down and why.
  3. Pin the numbers.p99 latency, peak TPS, availability, RPO/RTO. Agreed with the business before anyone builds.
  4. Own the integrations.Contracts with your core systems, vendors and government platforms, worked out with the team on the other side.
  5. Get it through security.Your security team sees the threat model before anything is built, and findings are closed before go-live.
  6. Make it boring to run.We don't sign off on go-live until dashboards, alerts, runbooks and a tested rollback are in place.

What we don't do

  • Write your code. Your engineers build it; we make sure it's the right thing, built the right way.
  • Run your backlog. That's your product owner's job.
  • Manage your people.
  • Pick your vendors. We write the technical evaluation, you make the call.
  • Go around your EA office. We follow your standards and push back in writing when one gets in the way.

How an engagement runs

  1. Weeks 1–2

    Listen first

    We go through the current architecture, the incident history and your recent postmortems, and sit with the business and ops. You get a map of what exists and a list of risks. No proposals yet.

  2. Weeks 3–4

    Decide on paper

    Requirements and target design as ADRs, NFRs in numbers, and integration contracts drafted with the teams on the other side.

  3. Week 5 on

    Steer the build

    Your squad builds; we keep it on the design. Riskiest integration first, while there's still time to be wrong.

  4. Every release

    Prove it

    Load test, alerts, runbooks and a rehearsed rollback before anything goes live.

  5. From day one

    Hand it over

    An engineer from your side pairs with the architect from the first ADR, so there's no big handover at the end.

What you keep when we leave

  • Requirements
  • ADRs
  • C4 diagrams
  • NFR sheet
  • Threat model
  • OpenAPI / AsyncAPI contracts
  • Runbooks

Nothing important lives in a slide deck or in our heads. If the system still needs us after handover, we count that as unfinished work, not a renewal.

  • Has led a squad from requirements to go-live
  • Has answered for systems where downtime costs real money
  • Has been through a regulator, a security review or an audit
  • Works in Arabic and English

Everyone on this page cleared all five.

Is this a fit?

Yes, if you've got

  • several squads, a hard date, and systems you can't switch off to deploy
  • integrations with payment rails, government platforms, or a core system older than some of your engineers
  • engineers and product owners, but nobody who owns the design and answers for it in production

Probably not, if you want

  • architecture for a tender that nobody plans to build
  • headcount without decision rights. An architect who can't decide is a note-taker.
  • a fixed scope handed to a vendor end to end. That's a systems integrator's job.
